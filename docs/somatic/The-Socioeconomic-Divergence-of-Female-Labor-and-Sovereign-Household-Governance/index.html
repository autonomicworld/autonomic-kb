2.1 The Orshansky 1963 Poverty Line Fallacy

The official U.S. poverty line ($31,200 for a family of four) is calculated using a formula developed by economist Mollie Orshansky in 19631. In 1963, groceries accounted for one-third of a family's budget, so Orshansky multiplied the minimum food budget by three to determine poverty1.

In 1963, this 3x multiplier worked because1:

  • Housing was cheap and reachable on a single wage.
  • Healthcare was provided by employers for ~$10/month.
  • Childcare did not exist as a market—mothers stayed home, and neighbors/relatives provided community support.

In 2026, food at home accounts for only 5% to 7% of household spending1. Housing consumes 35% to 45%, healthcare takes 15% to 25%, and childcare eats 20% to 40%1. If Orshansky’s methodology is updated to reflect modern spending realities, the food multiplier is no longer 3x; it is 16x1.

The true "income inadequacy" survival floor for a family of four in America is not $31,200. It is $136,500 to $140,000 gross income1.

The Closed Loop

To reach $80,000–$100,000 household income, most families require two earners1. But the moment the second earner enters the workforce, they trigger the $32,773 childcare expense, plus a second commuting car ($7,000+), work clothes, higher tax brackets, and lost healthcare subsidies1.

As Green notes, the second earner is not working for a luxury vacation or a boat. The second earner is working full-time just to pay the stranger at the daycare center so they can go to work and net a paltry $1,000–$2,000 extra per month1. It is a closed economic loop1.
